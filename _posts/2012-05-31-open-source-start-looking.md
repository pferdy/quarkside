Decades after successful use of open source by industry giants such as Google, Facebook and Apple, surely it is sufficient evidence to convince ICT managers that it should always be assessed.  Even the most conservative <a href="http://www.computerweekly.com/news/2240150985/Home-Office-saves-10m-opting-for-open-source" target="_blank">Home Office</a> is claiming benefits in £millions.

Cash strapped local government and voluntary sector agencies must start looking for savings by retiring legacy proprietary systems and moving to open source and open standards.  <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_source" target="_blank">Open Source</a> and <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_standard" target="_blank">Open Standards</a> are not the same thing, but they are often conflated - and sometimes with <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_data" target="_blank">Open Data</a>.  The key things to remember are:
<ul>
	<li>Open Source is free computer source code that is reusable and improvable.  Most users do not change the source code.</li>
	<li>Open Standards are the result of agreements of interested parties and encourage interoperability between systems.  Businesses should specify open standards in procurements, even for proprietary software.</li>
	<li>Open data is free to use, and should be defined as complying to an Open Standard.  It can be processed by Open Source or proprietary software.</li>
</ul>
